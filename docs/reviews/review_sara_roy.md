# Review: Certified Phase Atlases for Biological ODE Models

**Reviewer:** Sara Roy (Machine Learning for Formal Verification)
**Score: 6/10**

---

The ML component of this proposal—a Gaussian process surrogate for adaptive parameter-space exploration—is architecturally sound in its separation from the certification pipeline but thin in its ML methodology. The GP layer is explicitly described as "engineering infrastructure" rather than a research contribution, which is honest but also means the ML integration lacks the depth expected of a project at the intersection of machine learning and formal methods.

**Strengths.** The strict separation between GP-guided exploration (deciding *where* to look) and validated-numerics certification (deciding *what* to certify) is the correct design pattern for ML-integrated verification systems. This ensures that GP prediction errors—inevitable for complex phase boundaries—never contaminate the certified output. The phase-boundary-aware acquisition function directing sampling toward regions of high regime-transition probability is a reasonable application of Bayesian optimization to the parameter-space exploration problem. The claim that this reduces validated-integration calls compared to uniform grid search is plausible, as bifurcation boundaries are typically low-dimensional manifolds within the full parameter space. The GP-independent nature of all formal guarantees means the system degrades gracefully to uniform exploration if the GP model is poor—this is an important safety property for ML-augmented verification.

**Weaknesses.** The GP surrogate design is underspecified. The proposal does not discuss kernel selection (squared exponential, Matérn, or domain-specific alternatives), hyperparameter optimization strategy, or how the GP handles the categorical nature of regime labels (this is a classification problem, not regression—standard GP regression over continuous labels would need adaptation). The phase-boundary-aware acquisition function is not formally defined: is it an expected improvement variant, a knowledge gradient, or something custom? The 10× speedup target over uniform refinement at p = 4 is asserted without empirical or theoretical justification. The GP's training data consists of validated-integration outcomes, but the initial exploration strategy before the GP has sufficient training data is unspecified—a cold-start problem that could dominate total computation time for small benchmarks. The proposal does not compare against simpler exploration strategies (random sampling, Latin hypercube, Sobol sequences) that might achieve comparable speedup without the overhead of GP fitting and prediction. The optional GP layer (4,000 LoC) is small relative to the total codebase (48,000–72,000 LoC), suggesting it receives correspondingly less engineering attention. There is no discussion of GP scalability: for p ≤ 4, standard GP inference with O(n³) complexity in the number of training points is manageable, but for the stretch goal of p = 5–6 the GP may struggle with the curse of dimensionality in the input space. No uncertainty quantification is provided for the GP predictions—the acquisition function presumably uses GP posterior variance, but this is not stated. No analysis of how GP exploration interacts with the adaptive octree refinement is provided—do they operate at the same or different levels of the refinement hierarchy?

**Verdict.** The ML integration is architecturally correct but methodologically shallow. The GP surrogate needs explicit specification of kernel, acquisition function, and cold-start strategy, along with comparison against non-ML exploration baselines. As presented, the ML contribution is too thin to claim novelty in the ML-for-verification space—it is competent engineering infrastructure, consistent with how the authors describe it.
